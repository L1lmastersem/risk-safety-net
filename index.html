Sem Pater

I backtested 116 trading strategies. Most fell apart.

Almost every trading strategy you find online comes with a backtest that looks great. I rebuilt them myself in TradingView and Python, later also with machine learning, and tested every one the same way: real fees, no peeking at future data, and rules I wrote down before I saw the result. Most of them fell apart.

The one entry that kept working is the gap pullback. On crypto it was profitable every year from 2020 to 2026, including four years it was never built on, and it held up on the Nasdaq too. The Nasdaq version is now in a paper forward test.

This page shows a selection of what I tested, and the tool I built to check daily risk when several strategies trade on one account.

How I tested

  • No peeking ahead. Data from a higher timeframe is only used once that candle has closed. A backtest that can see the future always looks good.
  • Real costs. Exchange fees, overnight fees and slippage are always included. A result without fees doesn't count.
  • Rules first. I wrote down what counts as a pass before running the test, and didn't change settings after seeing the result.
  • Enough trades, in both halves. A result needs a real sample, and it has to hold up in the first and the second half of the data.
  • A holdout. Newer tests are built on older years and then run once on years they never saw, such as built on 2020–2023 and tested on 2024–2026.
  • A second market. Where possible, the same settings have to work on a symbol the strategy wasn't picked on.

Twelve of the results

Each row is drawn like a candle. The solid body is the profit factor after costs (everything won divided by everything lost), measured from 1.0: green makes money, red loses it. A thin wick shows what the strategy claimed. The dashed line at 1.3 is the usual bar to pass; the 6-year retest instead checked whether a result held up, year after year, on data it had never seen. What I took away from it

    A strategy can look safe on every chart separately and still break an account's daily loss limit when several symbols lose on the same day. Drop in trade lists exported from TradingView, or load the sample data, to see the real combined worst day and what a two-layer safety net does to it. Everything runs in your browser.

    How the safety net works. Layer 1 shrinks each new trade as the day's combined loss grows, so its worst case can't push the day past the limit. Layer 2 caps any single trade at a fixed share of the daily limit, which catches one oversized first trade of the day.

    How risk per trade is estimated. A trade export has each trade's outcome but not its original stop distance. The tool uses a losing trade's loss directly and half of a winning trade's gain as a rough stand-in, so treat the results as a direction, not exact to the dollar.

    About the sample data. Two made-up symbols over one year of trading days, generated in the browser. It is not real trading data.
